Machine Learning-Driven Airbnb Marketplace Optimisation

The Airbnb Analysis collaborative project aimed to identify distinct Airbnb listing profiles and evaluate their influence on price prediction, ultimately supporting business insights and actionable recommendations. The analysis integrated exploratory data analysis, feature preparation and engineering, clustering, regression modelling, and evaluation. Insights derived from these steps were intended to inform pricing strategies, targeted host recommendations, and marketplace positioning. The analysis of the NYC dataset, while acknowledging its limitations, demonstrated that clustering can reveal meaningful Airbnb listing profiles, but these groupings did not enhance price prediction accuracy. This suggests that identifying market-interpretable segments does not necessarily mean those groups should guide the predictive modelling strategy. Instead, listing-level signals may be more meaningful for a listing-specific, rather than segment-based, pricing approach.

From Industry 4.0 to 5.0: Toward a resilient, human-centric paradigm

The MGM ransomware attack highlights that while Industry 4.0 champions efficiency gains through automation and interconnectivity, these advances alone are insufficient. From a data integrity perspective, companies must ensure their data remains trustworthy before resuming operations, restarting personalised services, and building machine learning predictions. Artificial Intelligence (AI) can amplify data inaccuracies, causing substantial business risk. Industry 5.0 promotes an advanced ecosystem that integrates technology, data integrity, and operational reliability, with essential human oversight, sound judgment, and ongoing feedback. Adopting these practices supports informed decision-making, safeguards enterprises and individuals, and builds public trust in the underlying system.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

In the realm of big data, where vast volumes and complex structures can obscure meaningful patterns and introduce data quality issues, EDA becomes essential. It helps EDA enables analysts to understand datasets, evaluate data quality, discover hidden patterns, identify anomalies, and generate hypotheses. EDA is not a checklist but an iterative and progressive process. Each finding informs the next step and direction of analysis. New observations continually guide what to investigate next. Additionally, EDA supports the development of key questions for stakeholder discussions and helps challenge assumptions before advancing to feature selection and machine learning, ultimately supporting more informed business decisions.

From Correlation to Regression Models

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is closely linked to predictive modelling. Identifying and modelling relationships between variables helps inform model selection. Correlation measures the strength and direction of associations but does not indicate causation. A strong statistical relationship alone cannot prove that changes in one variable cause changes in another. This distinction is crucial during EDA, where correlation can reveal potential predictors, redundant variables, and multicollinearity, yet these findings must always be interpreted within the problem’s context. Linear, multiple linear, and polynomial regression model these relationships, generate predictions, and assess how well the models represent the underlying data.

Supervised Learning: Linear Regression

Understanding the progression from exploring relationships to modelling is essential. EDA identifies potential relationships, correlation quantifies their strength and direction, whereas linear regression extends this by modelling relationships between predictors and the target variable. As a supervised learning technique, linear regression fits a line that minimises the sum of squared residuals between actual and predicted values for a continuous target. However, model evaluation and residual analysis demonstrate that performance metrics alone do not provide a complete picture. Examining residuals helps determine whether prediction errors are randomly distributed or if the model has missed underlying patterns. Applying this to both Population–GDP and vehicle data helped connect the statistical concepts with practical model fitting, prediction and evaluation.

Clustering and Similarity Measures

Clustering is an unsupervised machine learning technique where data is not annotated or labelled with a class. This approach is used in various domains, such as marketing for customer grouping and medicine for patient segmentation. Observations are grouped based on similarity or distance without predefined labels. However, the definition of similarity, the data's characteristics, and the expected group structure all influence which clustering method is suitable. Different measures capture different notions of similarity, including Euclidean distance, SMC, Jaccard, Cosine, and Pearson. The data type is crucial when selecting a measure. For example, Jaccard works well for asymmetric binary attributes (0/1) where shared presence carries more information than shared absence. Therefore, there is no universal distance measure or clustering method that fits every dataset.

Unsupervised Learning - K-Means Clustering

K-Means is a simple and computationally efficient clustering technique that excels with numerical data, though categorical features require proper encoding. It performs best with compact, spherical clusters but may struggle with irregular shapes, varying feature scales, and outliers. K-Means requires specifying the number of clusters (k) in advance. The Elbow Method and Silhouette Score privide guidance but not definitive answers. Therefore, evaluation should extend beyond model fitting. The analytical judgment involves preparing features, selecting and validating k, evaluating the resulting clusters, and determining whether the segmentation has domain-specific meaning. Applying K-Means to the Wine dataset made this process practical, from exploring and standardising chemical attributes to selecting k, profiling the resulting clusters, and comparing the discovered structure with known wine categories.

Artificial Neural Networks: From Simple to Multilayer Perceptor

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) mimic how the brain’s neurons process signals, adjust weights, and learn to make predictions. I explored fundamental concepts from perceptrons to multilayer networks, including activation functions, error calculation, weight updates, and backpropagation. Exercises demonstrated how hidden layers allow ANNs to learn complex nonlinear patterns and highlighted key architectural choices. I applied these principles to Fashion-MNIST, using a multilayer perceptron, to address to a realistic multiclass clothing classification problem baased on the image dataset. Differences between training, validation, and test accuracy revealed overfitting and showed why training accuracy alone is inadequate for model evaluation.
