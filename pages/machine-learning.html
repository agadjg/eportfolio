model_training Machine Learning

This module began by surveying the broader landscape of machine learning and its influence on Industry 4.0 and 5.0. It then built a foundation in exploratory data analysis and statistical methods, such as correlation and regression, before moving on to clustering, neural networks, and natural language processing. Each topic combined practical application with an exploration of its underlying principles. The module concluded with model evaluation, deployment and the wider challenges shaping contemporary machine learning systems.

As McKinney (2022) highlighted and tutor feedback confirmed, data understanding and preparation—which take up 80% of project time—are crucial for analysis success. Working with datasets from housing, fuel consumption, and population-GDP to auto-mpg, I recognised how important this step is. I no longer view it as a fixed sequence of statistical tests and visualisation production. I treat it now as an iterative investigation of variable relationships and feature selection that informs preprocessing and modelling decisions. I learned that model complexity should adapt to data characteristics, but comparing regression models showed me that greater flexibility does not always improve generalisation (Géron, 2025; Tian and Zhang, 2022).

Clustering techniques revealed to me that the definition of similarity matters, and different measures (Euclidean, Jaccard, cosine) suit different data structures and analytical objectives. The wine-clustering exercise reinforced that groups emerge from feature preparation, scaling, and distance, and that cluster profiles, alongside statistical distinctiveness, should support their business relevance. However, the Airbnb project emphasised that identifying market-interpretable segments does not necessarily establish their predictive business value, raising a question between descriptive insights and predictive usefulness.

Working with neural networks and moving beyond code helped me understand what each training step does. It deepened my understanding of how models learn through iterative parameter updates. Initially, I was confused by manual training, high-level APIs, and mini-batch gradient descent. At first, I saw them as separate processes, but later realised they are all variations of the same learning cycle. Experimenting with learning rates taught me that a larger learning rate does not necessarily speed convergence or improve performance, and that a smaller one can slow progress.

My Fashion-MNIST activity demonstrated that learning only on training data is insufficient. Validation performance helps detect overfitting and guide model-setting choices, while the test set allows independent final assessment (Géron, 2025). This data separation is critical, as are the choice and interpretation of metrics. Choosing appropriate evaluation metrics requires considering the task at hand and the consequences of incorrect predictions (Novaković et al., 2017). My sentiment analysis showed how accuracy can conceal errors, and the CIFAR-10 image recognition activity reinforced that confidence scores and correctness differ. While these are just innocent examples, the real-world consequences can be severe, ranging from mispredicting cancer to misidentifying individuals. Therefore, evaluating model results requires balancing mathematical sophistication with careful human judgment, awareness of broader implications, and a sensitivity to human values.

Finally, I built on lessons from the Airbnb project—which explored listing profiles and their relationship with price—to my Sephora project on personalised skin product recommendations. Leveraging tutors' feedback from the team assignment, I connected model selection, validation, and subgroup performance to product discovery, retention, and explainability, and considered data bias, privacy, and algorithmic fairness. Reflecting on this experience, I recognise now that the quality of an ML project goes beyond producing a technically sound output. Translating them into coherent conclusions and actionable business insights is essential to producing a meaningful project outcome.

As I progressed through the module, I became more confident in understanding how machine learning works and more critical when interpreting its outputs, limitations and implications. As Nobel laureate Richard Feynman said, “What I cannot create, I do not understand” (Feynman, quoted in Foster, 2023, p. xvii) I began the module with this gap, and while it remains, it has narrowed.

This ePortfolio documents this journey, the questions, experiments and challenges that shaped my learning and developed my practical skills and judgement.

From Industry 4.0 to 5.0: Toward a resilient, human-centric paradigm

The MGM ransomware attack highlights that while Industry 4.0 champions efficiency gains through automation and interconnectivity, these advances alone are insufficient. From a data integrity perspective, companies must ensure their data remains trustworthy before resuming operations, restarting personalised services, and building machine learning predictions. Artificial Intelligence (AI) can amplify data inaccuracies, causing substantial business risk. Industry 5.0 promotes an advanced ecosystem that integrates technology, data integrity, and operational reliability, with essential human oversight, sound judgment, and ongoing feedback. Adopting these practices supports informed decision-making, safeguards enterprises and individuals, and builds public trust in the underlying system.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

In the realm of big data, where vast volumes and complex structures can obscure meaningful patterns and introduce data quality issues, EDA becomes essential. It helps EDA enables analysts to understand datasets, evaluate data quality, discover hidden patterns, identify anomalies, and generate hypotheses. EDA is not a checklist but an iterative and progressive process. Each finding informs the next step and direction of analysis. New observations continually guide what to investigate next. Additionally, EDA supports the development of key questions for stakeholder discussions and helps challenge assumptions before advancing to feature selection and machine learning, ultimately supporting more informed business decisions.

From Correlation to Regression Models

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is closely linked to predictive modelling. Identifying and modelling relationships between variables helps inform model selection. Correlation measures the strength and direction of associations but does not indicate causation. A strong statistical relationship alone cannot prove that changes in one variable cause changes in another. This distinction is crucial during EDA, where correlation can reveal potential predictors, redundant variables, and multicollinearity, yet these findings must always be interpreted within the problem’s context. Linear, multiple linear, and polynomial regression model these relationships, generate predictions, and assess how well the models represent the underlying data.

Supervised Learning: Linear Regression

Understanding the progression from exploring relationships to modelling is essential. EDA identifies potential relationships, correlation quantifies their strength and direction, whereas linear regression extends this by modelling relationships between predictors and the target variable. As a supervised learning technique, linear regression fits a line that minimises the sum of squared residuals between actual and predicted values for a continuous target. However, model evaluation and residual analysis demonstrate that performance metrics alone do not provide a complete picture. Examining residuals helps determine whether prediction errors are randomly distributed or if the model has missed underlying patterns. Applying this to both Population-GDP and vehicle data helped connect the statistical concepts with practical model fitting, prediction and evaluation.

Clustering and Similarity Measures

Clustering is an unsupervised machine learning technique where data is not annotated or labelled with a class. This approach is used in various domains, such as marketing for customer grouping and medicine for patient segmentation. Observations are grouped based on similarity or distance without predefined labels. However, the definition of similarity, the data's characteristics, and the expected group structure all influence which clustering method is suitable. Different measures capture different notions of similarity, including Euclidean distance, SMC, Jaccard, Cosine, and Pearson. The data type is crucial when selecting a measure. For example, Jaccard works well for asymmetric binary attributes (0/1) where shared presence carries more information than shared absence. Therefore, there is no universal distance measure or clustering method that fits every dataset.

Unsupervised Learning - K-Means Clustering

K-Means is a simple and computationally efficient clustering technique that excels with numerical data, though categorical features require proper encoding. It performs best with compact, spherical clusters but may struggle with irregular shapes, varying feature scales, and outliers. K-Means requires specifying the number of clusters (k) in advance. The Elbow Method and Silhouette Score privide guidance but not definitive answers. Therefore, evaluation should extend beyond model fitting. The analytical judgment involves preparing features, selecting and validating k, evaluating the resulting clusters, and determining whether the segmentation has domain-specific meaning. Applying K-Means to the Wine dataset made this process practical, from exploring and standardising chemical attributes to selecting k, profiling the resulting clusters, and comparing the discovered structure with known wine categories.

Machine Learning-Driven Airbnb Marketplace Optimisation

The Airbnb Analysis collaborative project aimed to identify distinct Airbnb listing profiles and evaluate their influence on price prediction, ultimately supporting business insights and actionable recommendations. The analysis integrated exploratory data analysis, feature preparation and engineering, clustering, regression modelling, and evaluation. Insights derived from these steps were intended to inform pricing strategies, targeted host recommendations, and marketplace positioning. The analysis of the NYC dataset, while acknowledging its limitations, demonstrated that clustering can reveal meaningful Airbnb listing profiles, but these groupings did not enhance price prediction accuracy. This suggests that identifying market-interpretable segments does not necessarily mean those groups should guide the predictive modelling strategy. Instead, listing-level signals may be more meaningful for a listing-specific, rather than segment-based, pricing approach.

Artificial Neural Networks: From Simple to Multilayer Perceptron

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are inspired by how the brain’s neurons receive and process signals, process information and transmit output. ANNs mimic this concept through input and weighted connections to learn to make predictions. I explored fundamental concepts from perceptrons to multilayer networks, including activation functions, error calculation, weight updates, and backpropagation. Activation functions are crucial for introducing nonlinearity, allowing multilayer networks with hidden layers to learn complex nonlinear patterns beyond what a linear perceptron can capture. I applied these principles to Fashion-MNIST with a multilayer perceptron. Differences between training, validation, and test accuracy revealed overfitting and showed why training accuracy alone is inadequate for model evaluation.

Training an Artificial Neural Network (ANN)

ANNs learn by minimising prediction errors, which are measured by a loss function that quantifies the difference between predicted and actual values. Backpropagation computes gradients that indicate how changes in each weight and bias impact the loss. Gradient descent then uses these gradients to update the parameters, with the learning rate determining the size of each adjustment. Through guided PyTorch exercises with the California Housing dataset, I practised building, training, and evaluating simple regression neural networks. I explored how the learning rate and iteration count affect convergence, aiming to reduce loss efficiently while preserving performance on unseen data. Experimenting with learning rates revealed that larger updates do not always enhance learning. Mini-batch training demonstrated that one epoch can involve multiple weight updates. These exercises reinforced the importance of evaluating performance on unseen data rather than relying solely on training loss.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) & Image Recognition

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are a class of deep learning algorithms that process images by applying filters (kernels) to extract and learn patterns from visual data. I explored image preparation, convolutional-layer filters, feature maps, activation functions, and pooling through guided PyTorch examples. Applying max-pooling demonstrated how reducing spatial dimensions can remove image details. During the CIFAR-10 exercise, I observed that an incorrectly labelled image could have a higher confidence score than a correctly labelled one. This highlights that confidence alone does not guarantee correctness. In facial recognition, such misidentifications can have serious consequences for individuals, emphasising the need for robust human-in-the-loop oversight. Effective supervision requires a deep understanding of a model's inner workings, development decisions, limitations, evaluation methods. These factors are especially crucial in high-stakes contexts, where model errors can significantly impact outcomes and decision-making.

Exploring Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Natural Language Processing (NLP) enables analysis and generation of human-like language. Early systems relied on rule-based approaches, statistical methods learned patterns from text input, and deep learning advanced the field by using neural networks to capture more complex language representations. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) process sequences step by step, while modern Transformers link information across tokens using attention mechanisms. Supervised learning uses pre-labelled examples, while self-supervised approaches learn from unlabeled data. BERT predicts masked tokens, while GPT predicts the next token. Pretraining lets models learn broad language representations, but it also risks learning biases and inaccuracies from the training data. Through practical experiments with BERT, GPT-2 and FLAN-T5, I explored masked-token prediction, text generation and summarisation. I learned that context and generation settings influence model outputs, but confidence and fluency do not guarantee correctness. Sentiment analysis further indicated that human review remains essential.

Model Selection and Performance Evaluation

Choosing a model requires matching the model to the task, class distribution, and weighing error costs. My classification exercises showed that accuracy alone can hide weaknesses, and metrics like precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity, and F1, along with a confusion matrix, can reveal error types. For regression tasks, MAE and MSE measure prediction errors, while R² compares performance with a mean-prediction baseline. Moreover, understanding bias and variance helped me connect model evaluation to improvement decisions. Training a model on data does not guarantee generalisability, and evaluating it on a validation set helps assess performance and guide choices. High training and validation errors can suggest underfitting, while a substantially lower training error than validation error can indicate overfitting. These patterns guide changes to model complexity, regularisation, training duration, or data. Separate test data should remain untouched for final evaluation.

AI Writers: From Confident-Sounding Fluency to Reliability

Today, LLMs support a wide range of administrative and creative work, delivering significant gains in efficiency and productivity. From summarising meetings to synthesising documents, writing books, and developing creative marketing campaigns, they produce engaging, innovative output. However, while these models can generate fluent content, they do not truly understand it. Because they rely on mathematical formulas rather than genuine comprehension of language or context, their performance can be unpredictable and error-prone. Mistakes become harder to spot as technology advances rapidly. Ultimately, humans must act as gatekeepers to safeguard and govern AI systems. This requires not only selecting appropriate metrics, assessing models in context, and fact-checking their output. It also means evaluating the emotional and societal impacts of LLMs and providing informed guidance on deployment decisions. Architectural advances alone do not guarantee reliability.

Sephora Recommender System

As product catalogues expand, customers face more choices, making it increasingly difficult to find relevant products. Can personalised recommendations help them discover items they are more likely to enjoy? This project explores whether customer ratings can support personalised Sephora product recommendations. A recommender system helps customers discover products they may like by learning from their past customer ratings to predict preferences and create personalised product rankings. This project compares classical and deep learning models—classical matrix factorisation, neural collaborative filtering, and feature-aware collaborative recommendation, to test whether added complexity improves product discovery. Model performance is evaluated in two ways: RMSE measures prediction accuracy, while NDCG@10 measures how well models rank relevant products near the top 10 results. SHAP is used to explain contributions to predicted ratings, alongside subgroup performance analysis and consideration of responsible deployment.
