functions Numerical Analysis

As Schober, Bossers, and Schwarte (2018) emphasise, combining descriptive and inferential statistics is essential for drawing meaningful conclusions. Ranganathan (2021) highlights that understanding the research question first is paramount. Next, she recommends analysing the data's characteristics and verifying statistical requirements—using formal tests and visual inspection—both of which are critical for valid inference. I learned that formulating a hypothesis depends on aligning with the data's underlying assumptions and understanding the type of data, the number of groups to be compared, and the relationships among them. This process guides the choice between parametric tests for comparing group means and non-parametric tests assessing the rank sums or rank differences.

However, these decisions are rarely linear. Different paths may be valid provided that assumptions are evaluated, test limitations are acknowledged, and the chosen method is well justified. For example, failing a normality test does not automatically rule out the use of a parametric test. In my blood pressure analysis, both the Mann-Whitney U test and the Welch t-test yielded the same statistical conclusion. As Fagerland (2012) notes, selecting the appropriate test is not a single-criterion decision. It requires visual inspection, assessment of outliers, and consideration of sample size and the test's robustness. Cross-validation strengthens confidence in these decisions.

Furthermore, these findings—supported by McShane et al. (2024)—confirm that statistical significance is not a simple, binary decision. Greenland et al. (2016) and Tijssen (2021) argue that the p-value alone does not convey meaningful information. While it provides evidence against the null hypothesis, it does not address the magnitude, precision, or practical significance of an observed effect, which are captured by the confidence interval.

My R activities confirmed the existence of this gap. In my multilinear regression model, one variable had a borderline p-value near the 0.05 significance level. The wide confidence interval showed the effect was real, but the effect size was imprecise. Conversely, a demographic predictor was significant in a logistic model but became insignificant after adding another variable. The odds ratio and a narrow confidence interval indicated that the effect was precise but negligible. These examples demonstrate why considering the full picture reveals insights, strengthens conclusions and avoids misinterpretations.

Additionally, summary and contingency tables form the foundation of analysis. While extracting summary output in R is straightforward, presenting it meaningfully requires careful consideration. Choosing between means/standard deviations and medians/interquartile ranges for continuous variables, and between counts, percentages, or both for categorical variables, should always align with the purpose of analysis.

Contingency tables, meanwhile, prompt important questions about how categorical variables are allocated. I came to understand that their analytical value and interpretation depend on their organisation and layout, which should be guided by the research question rather than by R's default output.

Finally, I realised that creating graphical visualisations is more than just producing graphs. My experience and peer feedback—such as from Brown's forum discussion —taught me that careful data preparation, thoughtful selection, and simplicity are key. The abundance of graphic choices challenged my restraint, and I learned the importance of aligning visuals to the audience's graphic literacy. My perspective shifted from viewing graphs as mere outputs to appreciating them as intelligent analytical reasoning tools. As emphasised by Balan (2024), when used effectively, visualisation promotes understanding, powerfully conveys a clear message, builds trust with the reader, and aids decision-making.

Why is the p-value not enough?

Reporting statistics truthfully and transparently, while acknowledging uncertainty in decision-making, is neither simple nor straightforward. Although the p-value indicates statistical significance, it does not convey the magnitude, precision, or practical significance of an observed effect. The key aspects are reflected by the confidence interval. Adopting a holistic approach is essential to minimise misinterpretation and support effective clinical decision-making.
